About the Initiative

Who we are & why this matters

Stop Killing Games is a consumer rights campaign born from a simple observation: publishers are legally destroying products that people paid real money for — and nothing is being done about it.

Origin

How this campaign began

Ross Scott had been documenting the practice of publishers destroying games for years — cataloguing titles that had been made permanently unplayable due to server shutdowns, removed DRM infrastructure, or service terminations. What started as personal frustration became a structured, evidence-based campaign.

The core insight was straightforward: consumers in every other industry have legal protections against being sold products that later become non-functional through deliberate action by the seller. Video games had no such protection.

Rather than simply criticising the industry, Ross Scott committed to building an organised campaign — one with realistic, legally achievable demands, grounded in existing consumer rights frameworks. The result was Stop Killing Games.

The initiative is non-partisan and non-commercial. It does not oppose online gaming or subscription services. It only requires that, when a publisher ends a game's service, they leave it in a state where the consumer can still play what they purchased.

Mission

What we stand for

The mission of Stop Killing Games is to establish legal norms that protect consumers from having purchased products deliberately destroyed by their creators. We believe that when a consumer purchases a video game, they are entitled to the reasonable expectation that the product will remain usable — not indefinitely, but at minimum in a functional offline or emulated state after the publisher ends its commercial support.

This is not a radical position. It reflects how consumer rights work in virtually every other product category, and it is entirely achievable through standard legislative mechanisms — which is why the initiative has chosen the European Citizens' Initiative and equivalent mechanisms as its vehicle.

Goals

Four things we are working to achieve

These are concrete, proportionate policy goals grounded in existing consumer rights principles.

Functional End-of-Service

Publishers must release patches, server emulation tools, or other mechanisms that allow a purchased game to remain playable after service ends — at no additional cost to consumers.

Adequate Advance Notice

Consumers must be informed well in advance when a game will lose functionality, with sufficient time to seek refunds or alternative options before the title becomes inaccessible.

Legal Obligation

The requirement to leave games playable must be enshrined in law, with clear enforcement mechanisms and penalties for publishers who deliberately destroy products consumers have purchased.

Right to Repair & Host

Where publishers decline to provide solutions, consumers and third parties should have the legal right to maintain, repair, or host the game infrastructure necessary for continued play.
